# Source Credibility and Search Strategies

## Source Tiers

Every claim must be tagged with its source tier. This lets the reader weight confidence.

### Tier 1: Authoritative (High Trust)

- Academic papers in peer-reviewed journals
- Official government data and statistics (census, regulatory filings)
- Primary legal documents (laws, court rulings, patents)
- Official documentation from the entity being researched
- Pre-registered clinical trials and systematic reviews

**When to use**: As foundation for claims. Always prefer Tier 1 when available.

### Tier 2: Reputable Journalism (Good Trust)

- Established news outlets with editorial oversight (NYT, Reuters, BBC, etc.)
- Industry-specific publications with domain expertise
- Investigative reporting with named sources and methodology
- Books from established publishers with citations

**When to use**: For recent events, industry trends, and topics where academic research hasn't caught up. Verify claims across 2+ Tier 2 sources when Tier 1 is unavailable.

### Tier 3: Expert Analysis (Moderate Trust)

- Recognized experts writing in their domain (blog posts, LinkedIn articles, talks)
- Industry analyst reports (Gartner, Forrester, McKinsey)
- Well-cited Wikipedia articles (check the citations, not the article text)
- Conference presentations with supporting data

**When to use**: For context, opinions, and predictions. Useful for understanding the landscape but verify factual claims against Tier 1-2.

### Tier 4: Community Sources (Low Trust)

- Reddit, HackerNews, forum discussions
- Social media posts (X/Twitter, YouTube comments)
- Anonymous or pseudonymous sources
- AI-generated content without citations

**When to use**: Only for understanding sentiment, finding leads to better sources, or discovering edge-case experiences. Never cite as evidence for factual claims.

## Search Query Strategies

Don't just search once and read. Use different query strategies to uncover different facets.

### Angle Rotation

Each search round should approach from a different angle:

| Round | Strategy | Example (topic: "electric car reliability") |
|-------|----------|----------------------------------------------|
| 1 | Direct factual | "electric car reliability statistics 2024 2025" |
| 2 | Counter-argument | "electric car problems reliability issues" |
| 3 | Expert opinion | "mechanic electric car maintenance costs" |
| 4 | Academic | "EV battery degradation study longitudinal" |
| 5 | User experience | "electric car owner long-term review reliability" |
| 6 | Comparative | "electric vs gas car total cost of ownership study" |

### Query Operators

- `site:` — restrict to specific domains (site:gov, site:edu, site:reuters.com)
- `"exact phrase"` — force exact match for technical terms
- `before:YYYY-MM-DD after:YYYY-MM-DD` — time-bounded search for recent developments
- `-term` — exclude noise (e.g., `-reddit -quora` for more authoritative results)
- `filetype:pdf` — find research papers and official documents

### Source Discovery

When initial searches surface a key source:

1. Check what that source **cites** — follow the citation chain to primary sources
2. Check what **cites that source** — find responses, critiques, and updates
3. Check the **author's other work** — are they a recognized domain expert?
4. Check the **publication date** — is this still current? Has anything changed since?

## Contradiction Assessment

When two credible sources contradict:

1. **Check recency** — newer source may have better data
2. **Check methodology** — one may have a larger sample size or better controls
3. **Check scope** — they may be measuring different things in different contexts
4. **Check funding** — industry-funded research has a known bias pattern
5. **Check consensus** — if 8 of 10 sources agree, note the 2 dissenters but weight accordingly

Present contradictions as: "Source A [Tier N] claims X based on [method]. Source B [Tier M] claims Y based on [method]. The likely reason for disagreement is [analysis]. The weight of evidence leans toward [position] because [reasoning]."
